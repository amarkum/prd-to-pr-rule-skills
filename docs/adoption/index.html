Agentic SDLC Adoption Plan

A 12-month plan, starting Monday 26 October 2026, to turn AI-tool adoption into measurable value per builder. One accountable builder owns each change. Every phase produces a small reviewed artifact. Agents do most of the work inside guardrails written as code, and people spend their attention on judgement.

The plan on one screen

  1. W0 · 26 Oct–6 NovMobilize. Review the plan with the executive sponsor, name and approve a task force of 6–7, lock the baseline from internal analytics, and inventory every agent teams have built so they merge into one framework.
  2. W1 · Nov 2026Standardize and instrument. 27 → 40 of 48 repos on the standard, AI code share and feature-level records live, managed settings for every builder, and framework v0.5 in daily use by the pilot team.
  3. W2 · Dec–JanFramework v1 and expansion. One agent framework, skills-first and packaged as a plugin, by Fri 18 Dec. The pilot team moves to “agent drafts the intent and the PR, a human reviews the PR”. 48/48 repos and the first backend cohort by Fri 29 Jan.
  4. W3 · Feb–AprAutomated gates and scale. Second backend cohort, required gates on every repo, multi-agent PR review and a release agent up to staging. The first human gate is retired once its automated replacement has earned it.
  5. W4 · May–JulEvery team on the workflow, and the product-builder role made official in the career framework.
  6. W5 · Jun–OctClose the loop with SRE diagnosis and incident agents on an explicit autonomy ladder, then review ROI on measured numbers. Readout on Mon 1 Nov 2027.

Assumptions

  • From the company plan: 48 repos in scope with 27 on the standard, one team piloting the workflow, and a 1.3x productivity multiple against a 10x aspiration.
  • Company targets: 40/48 repos by 30 Nov, 48/48 by 29 Jan, agent framework from December. Targets marked proposed are confirmed on Mon 26 Oct.
  • Biweekly reviews with the executive sponsor every other Monday from 26 Oct. Per-team baselines are still to be locked from internal analytics.
  • Claude features below were checked against Claude Code 2.1.302 and its docs on 23 Oct 2026.

1What I understood

The thesis in plain words

Adopting AI is different from getting value from it. Most organizations now use AI coding tools every day. Few can show that they deliver more value per person or per unit of cost. Tool usage, lines of code and PR counts show that activity happened. They do not show that customers got more.

Faster coding moves the bottleneck. When agents make code cheap, the waiting moves to everything around the code: unclear requirements, design decisions, finding the standards, human review, security and compliance checks, deployment approvals and incident analysis. Speed up only the coding step and the system stays almost as slow as before.

The root cause is the design of the organization. Work travels through a relay: product manager, designer, developer, tester, lead reviewer, release team. Every handoff loses context, adds a queue, causes rework and spreads ownership thin. AI makes each runner faster while the relay stays slow. So the model collapses roles around one accountable builder and redesigns the whole lifecycle: agents do the work, deterministic gates constrain them, and humans judge, approve and stay accountable.

Two effects make this urgent. AI amplifies the system it lands in: a team with clear ownership, good standards and fast feedback gets faster, while a fragmented team produces more activity and the same value. And there is a trust paradox: people feel more productive while trusting the output less, which raises the need for validation, automated gates and observability.

The single accountable builder

One person owns a change from requirement to release readiness. They do not work alone: agents, automated gates, domain specialists, reviewers, standards, test frameworks and release controls support them. They are accountable for knowing why the change matters, who it affects, what success means, what the risks are, how it will be validated and whether it is ready to release. That is a product engineer, an outcome owner rather than a ticket taker.

Decisions the vision asks of leadership

  1. Adopt the artifact chain: intent, spec, plan, verification evidence, release manifest, production feedback.
  2. Define the product-builder role and what builders own.
  3. Move from activity metrics to value metrics.
  4. Standardize engineering repositories.
  5. Treat agent behaviour as production behaviour.

Section 8 folds them into twelve decisions, each with a recommendation and the date it is needed.

Definition of success

We have succeeded when one accountable builder, working with agents and automated gates, takes a short requirement for a defined class of change to a released and adopted feature, and the numbers show more impact per builder than the baseline with no loss of quality or safety.

2Risks and design principles

The direction of the vision is sound. These are the places where it fails in practice, and the principle this plan uses against each one.

R1No baseline, so nothing can be proven

What goes wrong. A year from now someone asks whether output per person went up. Without numbers from before the change, every answer is an opinion, and good results look like seasonality or new hires.

Principle. Measure before changing anything. Freeze a baseline window (May–Oct 2026), lock the metric definitions, and keep a comparison team that does not change during W2–W3.

Wave 0 · metrics/feature-record.schema.json

R2The full chain on every change becomes process theater

What goes wrong. Six artifacts for a typo fix teaches people that the process is paperwork. They fill templates with boilerplate, and the real thinking moves back into chat.

Principle. Proportional process. Tier the chain by change class (table below). Agents draft every artifact and humans review. Measure artifact overhead and delete sections nobody reads.

W1–W2 tuning · docs/changes/README.md · scripts/check_artifacts.py

R3Human review becomes the new bottleneck

What goes wrong. Agents produce more and bigger PRs than people can read. Review queues grow, reviewers skim, and quality falls exactly where it was supposed to rise.

Principle. Review by risk, not by line. Agents absorb style, simple bugs, coverage and standard policy. Humans judge problem fit, architecture, blast radius and residual risk. Cap parallel sessions at what people can review, keep PRs small, and track review wait as a flow metric.

W2–W3 · review-synthesizer · claude-pr-review.yml

R4Agents game the tests

What goes wrong. Under pressure to go green, an agent skips a test, loosens an assertion, deletes a case or special-cases the test input. The dashboard improves and the product gets worse.

Principle. Tests are a contract. Freeze defect tests, block edits to them with a hook, ask a human before any skip marker lands, check frozen files again in CI, verify independently, and keep eval cases that fail when an agent cheats. Hooks catch the obvious; evals and review catch the subtle.

W1 · test_guard.py · check_frozen_tests.py · EVAL-0001

R5Roles and incentives are the hardest part

What goes wrong. The tooling works and nothing changes, because performance reviews still reward tickets closed, and QA, PM and release specialists see the plan as a threat to their jobs.

Principle. Design the role with the people in it. Co-write the product-builder role with the pilot team, give QA, PM and release specialists explicit new paths (gates, evals, policy packs, discovery), and change what reviews reward before scaling.

W2 draft · W4 career framework · decision D12

R6Goodhart's law on the metrics

What goes wrong. A measure that becomes a target stops measuring. Teams split changes to raise counts, reclassify defects as features, or game cycle time by opening PRs late.

Principle. Pair every metric with a counter-metric (speed with change failure rate, output with impact, cost with success). Report at team level only, lock definitions for a quarter, and read the numbers next to retro notes.

Section 6 · paired metrics

R7Agents get access to credentials and production

What goes wrong. A session inherits a developer's cloud profile, reads a .env, or is talked into running a deploy. One prompt injection or one confused command reaches production.

Principle. Agents never hold production credentials. Dev, QA and staging credentials are short-lived and scoped. Bash runs in a sandbox with a network allowlist, secret files are denied by managed settings, and a production-boundary hook backs this up.

W1 · managed settings · prod_boundary_guard.py

R8Empowering before standardizing

What goes wrong. The vision lists Stage 3 (Empower) before Stage 4 (Standardize). Removing human approvals before automated gates exist removes the only control there is.

Principle. Replace, don't remove. Stage 4 starts in W1. A human gate is retired only after its automated replacement has run in shadow with measured agreement (see below).

W1 → W3 · shadow log

R9Agent sprawl and silent drift

What goes wrong. Every team writes its own agents and prompts. A model update or a prompt tweak changes behaviour and nobody notices until something escapes.

Principle. Six core agents and shared skills from one plugin. Agent configuration changes by PR and must pass the eval suite, which also runs weekly to catch model drift.

W1 plugin · agent-evals.yml · decision D3

R10Cost without a ceiling, or a ceiling that kills value

What goes wrong. Agent compute grows without anyone watching, or finance caps it so hard that builders stop using agents for the work that pays.

Principle. A monthly envelope per builder set from telemetry, hard caps on automated runs, and one number that matters: cost per successful change, not cost per token.

Decision D8 · OpenTelemetry · --max-budget-usd

R11Teams keep building agents on their own

What goes wrong. Security, operations and requirements teams each ship their own agent. Effort is duplicated, behaviour differs from team to team, and nobody can evaluate or govern the whole.

Principle. One framework. Inventory what exists, merge the useful parts into shared skills and subagents, and make their owners contributors. No standalone agents after Fri 6 Nov.

W0 · tracker 1.5 · decision D3

R12The program depends on one person

What goes wrong. Every decision and every change routes through the transformation lead, so progress stops whenever the lead is in meetings, on leave or pulled into other work.

Principle. A task force with a named owner for every area, a framework deputy, and a tracker that anyone can run the stand-up from.

W0 · tracker 1.2–1.4 · decisions D2, D5

Replace, don't remove: how a human gate is retired

W1–W3Build the automated gatemake verify, the artifact check, frozen-test check and multi-agent review, all as required CI checks.
W3 · from Mon 1 FebRun it in shadowThe human gate still decides. Every PR logs both decisions so agreement and misses can be measured.
W3 · by Fri 30 AprRetire the human gate for one classOnly when agreement is at least 90% over 4+ weeks and no blocking issue was missed. Significant and high-risk classes keep their human gates.

Design principles

  1. Baseline before change. No wave starts without the numbers it will be judged on.
  2. Proportional process. Artifacts scale with risk, and agents draft them.
  3. Replace, don't remove. A human gate goes only when an automated gate has a track record.
  4. Review by risk. Humans judge fit, architecture, blast radius and residual risk.
  5. Tests are a contract. Frozen tests, guard hooks, independent verification, evals.
  6. Paired, team-level metrics. No individual leaderboards in year one.
  7. No production credentials for agents. The boundary is identity and sandbox; hooks are a seatbelt.
  8. Agent configuration is production code. Versioned, reviewed, evaluated, distributed from one place.
  9. People first. Roles and incentives are designed with the people who hold them.

6Measurement model

One record per change, from first signal to confirmed impact, feeds every number below. Report medians and 85th percentiles per change class, at team level, with definitions locked for a quarter.

Question 1: is output per person really increasing?

Primary measure: impact-qualified changes per builder per quarter. A change counts when it reached production and met the impact threshold its intent defined (adoption, reduced effort, revenue, savings, fewer defects or tickets). Each change is weighted by class so that splitting work does not inflate the count.

IQC per builder-quarter = Σ weight(class) over changes with impact.threshold_met
                          ÷ builder FTE-quarters

weight(class): trivial 0 · standard 1 · significant 3 · high-risk 5   (calibrate in W0 from median effort per class)

Read it next to its guardrails: cost per successful change, escaped defects per change, change failure rate, review hours per change, and how builders report their own toil. Output that rises while a guardrail falls is not a gain.

Attribution: compare the pilot team with its own baseline period and with a comparison team that does not change in W2–W3. Say plainly what cannot be attributed to the program.

AI code share in production stays the company's headline metric. Measure it as lines in AI-authored commits merged to main divided by all merged lines, using Claude Code's commit attribution and telemetry joined with git. Read it next to the guardrails above: a rising share counts as progress only while quality and impact hold.

Question 2: how does our structure compare with a leaner AI-native model?

Three structural measures, tracked as trends rather than headcount targets.

  • Handoff depth: distinct human roles that worked on or approved a change between approved intent and production. Automated and policy gates do not count.
  • Single-builder share: share of non-trivial production changes with a handoff depth of 1, where every other check was automated or a policy gate.
  • Layers and span: management layers between a builder and the CTO, builders per manager, and builders per coordinating role (product, QA, release, delivery management), from HR data.

Without this, a rise in feature output can simply mean that defect work was deferred. Claude classifies every work item from the baseline window (prompt B3), a human audits a stratified sample of 40, and the shares are reported by count and by elapsed days.

ROI

Annual benefit  B = (IQC_now − IQC_baseline) × builders × 4 × value per weighted change
                  + escaped defects avoided × cost per defect
                  + incident minutes avoided × cost per minute
Annual cost     C = agent compute and licences + enablement team + training and transition dip
ROI = (B − C) ÷ C          Payback in months = C ÷ (B ÷ 12)

Value per weighted change starts as a conservative proxy, the loaded cost of the effort that change took at baseline productivity, and is replaced by measured impact as adoption data matures. Try the formula with your own numbers:

Rules that keep the metrics honest

  • Every speed metric has a quality partner, and every output metric has an impact partner.
  • Team level only. No individual leaderboards in year one.
  • Definitions change at most once a quarter, with the change logged.
  • Activity metrics (sessions, lines of AI code, PR counts) stay operational signals and never become targets.

11What not to do

  • noReport adoption as progress. Seats, sessions and lines of AI code are preconditions, not results.
  • noRequire all six artifacts for every change. Tier by class or the chain becomes paperwork.
  • noRemove a human gate before its replacement has a track record in shadow mode.
  • noGive agents production credentials, not even temporarily, not even read-write "just for this incident".
  • noBuild dozens of bespoke agents. Improve the six core agents with evals instead.
  • noChange prompts, skills, hooks or models without running the evals.
  • noPublish individual productivity leaderboards. They turn every metric into a target.
  • noScale before the pilot's own numbers hold against the baseline and the comparison team.
  • noAsk humans to read every generated line. Review by risk.
  • noLet agents freeze or unfreeze tests. Only humans edit the frozen list.
  • noSkip the baseline because "we know we are slow". You will need the number in month 12.
  • noRun more parallel sessions than people can review. Start at two or three.
  • noLet templates grow. Delete any section nobody reads.
  • noClimb to L4 or L5 without a track record at the level below.
  • noBuild agents outside the shared framework. Contribute to it instead.
  • noLet the tracker go stale. Update it every Friday; every review starts from it.
